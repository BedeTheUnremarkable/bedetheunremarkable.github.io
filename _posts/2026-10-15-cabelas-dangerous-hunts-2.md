**Rating: 5.5/10**

*Cabela's Dangerous Hunts 2* is not really a hunting simulation. It's a budget FPS with natural horror themes, and I think approaching it as anything else is setting yourself up for disappointment. There's very little hunting strategy involved, and it's closer to one of those arcade hunting games you'd find at an actual Cabela's store, except someone decided to make it dark and edgy. Imagine if *Chicken Shoot* took itself seriously as a horror game, and it actually worked to some degree. That's more or less what you're getting here.

The voice acting is mediocre, although not intolerable, and has a certain B-movie charm to it. A lot of the comedy is obviously unintentional, but I think there's a distinction worth making between something being so bad it's good in a cutscene and something being so bad it's good in the actual gameplay. The former is often charming, whereas the latter can just be miserable to play. Fortunately, *Dangerous Hunts 2* is more competent at the latter than you might expect, provided you're willing to work around some of its questionable design decisions.

I initially played the game on normal difficulty, and while I enjoyed some of its atmosphere, I found the actual encounters underwhelming. Your character is ridiculously durable, health regenerates, and the slowdown mechanic is so powerful that being ambushed by several predators at once is rarely as frightening as it ought to be. A cougar jumping out of the bushes at you should be a potentially devastating experience, not a minor inconvenience you recover from shortly afterwards.

After finishing it, I played through it again on hard, this time with the music turned off and hardly ever using the slowdown mechanic. This improved the experience considerably. The game is much more threatening when its animals can actually kill you, you can't trivialize every attack by slowing down time, and the environmental sounds are allowed to do the work of establishing the atmosphere. I'd go so far as to recommend playing it this way on your first playthrough.

The first level actually has some remarkably atmospheric moments, particularly the cougar ambushes. The audio cues can get excessive, especially if you're progressing quickly, but there were several occasions where I was genuinely startled. I know jumpscares are widely disliked as a cheap way of frightening people, but I think they're entirely appropriate here. You're wandering through wilderness inhabited by predators, some of which are actively stalking you. An animal suddenly emerging from the undergrowth to attack you is precisely the sort of thing that *should* be frightening in a game like this.

The second level is probably where the game shines the most, especially on hard. Ammunition is scarce, and you can find yourself fighting off big cats with little more than a machete. Barely making it out of an encounter alive is considerably more exciting than effortlessly shooting everything that moves. The scrub buffalo gauntlet in Australia is another highlight. There's something genuinely appealing about this sort of frantic, desperate combat, even when the underlying shooting mechanics are simplistic.

Unfortunately, the animals don't behave differently enough from one another. I don't particularly care if animal attacks in a natural horror game are unrealistic, but a hyena and a big cat shouldn't attack you in almost exactly the same fashion. There's considerable potential for making different species frightening in different ways, and *Dangerous Hunts 2* doesn't do much with it. This is particularly disappointing because the encounters themselves can be effective when the difficulty is high enough.

The level design is also extremely linear, although I don't think that's entirely to the game's detriment. The objectives are repetitive, and after the first mission they barely matter because you're generally just following the path in front of you anyway. Curiously, I think the absence of a map was an inspired choice. There's something to be said for making the player rely on their surroundings in a game attempting this sort of atmosphere. I've always thought the Jaguar version of *Alien vs. Predator* would have benefited from something similar. Seriously, the Predator is the only character who should have a map in that game!

Then there's the story, which is where things get particularly interesting.

The writing is very much a product of 2005, and you can make your own judgment about its depictions of Indigenous peoples. I don't think it's anything particularly egregious, although Wirake's manner of speaking is probably the worst of it. What's amusing is that the actual gameplay has very little to do with the supernatural beliefs being discussed in the cutscenes. You're mostly escorting people through dangerous country, which seems like a considerably more believable activity for a grizzled wilderness survivalist and rescue specialist anyway.

That said, I actually like the idea of the supposedly outlandish stories told to an outsider turning out to be true. It's a neat reversal of the usual setup, even if the execution is rather clumsy. It's also inherently funny that a series licensed by a sporting-goods retailer has an interconnected supernatural mythology. The bear you kill in the 2003 game apparently returns in *Dangerous Hunts 2011* with a grudge against the family. Someone probably bought one of these games for their granddad because he likes hunting, and now he's involved in a multigenerational supernatural revenge story.

There's also something amusing about the game's peculiar approach to animal welfare. At various points you're required to use a tranquilizer gun on elephants, rhinoceroses, and tigers, but you're apparently free to hack lions to death with a machete. I suppose even supernatural hunting adventures have to draw the line somewhere.

However, I discovered something interesting during my second playthrough: *Dangerous Hunts 2* is almost a different game if you skip the cutscenes.

With them enabled, it's a campy B-movie adventure with mediocre acting, dated writing, and an increasingly ridiculous plot culminating in a fight against a yeti. The yeti is practically a punchline, the final escalation in a game that has already become rather silly.

Without the cutscenes, however, it's a surprisingly bleak wilderness survival game in which you're escorting people through dangerous environments, struggling with ammunition shortages, and being ambushed by hostile wildlife. When the yeti finally appears, it doesn't necessarily feel like the punchline to an absurd story anymore. It's more like a revelation that the people who claim to have seen Bigfoot were telling the truth all along.

Neither interpretation is necessarily wrong, but they pull in opposite directions. The B-movie charm and the genuinely effective horror are separate layers of the experience, and to some extent you can choose which one you want. I actually think the game is scarier without its story, which is a rather peculiar thing to say about a horror game.

On a technical note, I strongly recommend playing the PC version rather than the PS2 release. The PC game needs some fixes to run properly on modern hardware, but they do exist, and I'm surprised anyone cared enough to make them for something this obscure. I initially considered switching to the PS2 version because of the stretched image on PC, but the gamepad controls were so much worse that I went looking for another solution. Gamescope eventually allowed me to pillarbox the game properly, and I had a much better time with it. Seriously, I cannot overstate how much worse this game feels with a controller, especially on hard. Use a mouse and keyboard.

The whole experience lasts around three and a half hours, which I actually think is an appropriate length. The game is repetitive enough that stretching it out to eight hours probably would have made it considerably more tiresome, and given that so much of its horror relies on sudden ambushes, I suspect the novelty would have worn off long before then. It's short enough to be enjoyable without overstaying its welcome.

I've developed something of an appreciation for middling hunting games, and I think their appeal is comparable to that of middling zombie games like *Dead Island* or *Land of the Dead: Road to Fiddler's Green*. There are certain atmospheres and scenarios that relatively few video games attempt, and sometimes a thoroughly average game can be worth playing simply because it offers something you're unlikely to find elsewhere. I understand why people have nostalgia for the old Cabela's games, but I don't think their appeal is exclusively nostalgic. I'm coming to these games without that attachment, and I can still appreciate what they're trying to do.

Ultimately, I think the 2.5/5 rating on Backloggd is about right for *Dangerous Hunts 2*. It's not particularly good as a hunting game, and it's not an especially accomplished FPS either. The mechanics are simplistic, the missions are repetitive, the writing is ridiculous, and some of its design decisions actively undermine what the game does well. I also don't think requiring the player to turn off the music, avoid using a major mechanic, and play on hard to get the most out of it is something that deserves praise.

But there are moments here where it genuinely works as natural horror, particularly when you're low on ammunition, surrounded by hostile wildlife, and uncertain whether you'll survive the next encounter. That's more than I expected from a budget hunting game made by a studio obscure enough not to have its own Wikipedia page.

I wouldn't recommend it to someone looking for a good hunting simulation or a particularly satisfying shooter. But if you're interested in natural horror, or just have an appreciation for strange, unremarkable games that occasionally manage to do something memorable, I think it's worth three or four hours of your time.

It's fine. And sometimes fine things have their appeal.
